Ethereum just crossed a milestone that would have seemed impossible two years ago. In the first quarter of 2026, the network processed 200.4 million base-layer transactions -- the first time quarterly activity has ever broken the 200 million mark, and more than double the lows recorded in 2023.

Activity jumped 43% from Q4 2025's 145 million transactions. Active addresses surged by more than 1,700% over the same period. By raw usage numbers, Ethereum is experiencing the busiest period in its history.

So why is ETH still down more than 50% from its August 2025 peak near $5,000?

That question sits at the heart of crypto's most interesting debate right now: the relationship between network activity and token value.

## What Is Driving the Activity Surge

The growth is not coming from a single source. A few structural trends are converging.

Layer 2 networks -- Arbitrum, Base, Optimism, and others -- now route enormous volumes through Ethereum's base layer for settlement and data availability. Every L2 batch posted to Ethereum counts as a transaction, and as L2 adoption has grown, so has the number of these settlement calls on L1.

Stablecoin transfers are another major driver. The stablecoin market has grown significantly, and a large share of that activity settles on Ethereum. Cross-border transfers, DeFi collateral movements, and institutional payroll applications all generate consistent transaction volume.

Finally, tokenized real-world assets have added a new category of on-chain activity. Tokenized treasuries, money market funds, and private credit instruments now settle portions of their operations on Ethereum, creating steady baseline volume that did not exist in previous cycles.

## The Fee Problem

Here is where the narrative gets complicated. The 2024 Dencun upgrade dramatically reduced the cost of posting data to Ethereum from Layer 2 networks. That was the point -- cheaper rollups mean more users can afford to transact. But the tradeoff is that L2 batches now generate less fee revenue for L1 validators, and less ETH gets burned through the fee mechanism introduced in EIP-1559.

Before Dencun, a spike in L2 activity would have translated into higher L1 fees, more ETH burned, and net deflationary pressure on the supply. That loop is now partially broken. High transaction counts no longer automatically mean high fees, and high fees are what drive deflationary ETH issuance.

The result is a network that looks busier than ever on the surface but generates less economic activity per transaction than in previous cycles.

## What the Glamsterdam Upgrade Could Change

Ethereum developers are targeting a Glamsterdam hard fork for June 2026. One of its goals is to improve fee market dynamics for L2s, potentially restoring some of the value flow from L2 activity back to the L1. If successful, it could tighten the relationship between network usage and token economics.

There is also a longer-term proposal to dramatically increase blob capacity, which would let more L2 transactions settle on L1 at even lower cost. That would push transaction counts higher again, but fee revenue implications remain unclear.

## The Divergence Is a Signal, Not Just a Puzzle

The gap between Ethereum's usage records and its token price tells us something important about how crypto is maturing. In earlier cycles, activity and price moved together because the network had fewer users and the fee mechanism was simpler. Now the system is larger, more layered, and harder to read from price alone.

Standard Chartered recently set a $40,000 price target for ETH, pointing partly to the transaction record as evidence of fundamental demand. Whether that view holds depends on whether the upcoming protocol changes can convert activity into fee pressure.

For now, Ethereum's Q1 numbers confirm one thing clearly: people are using the network at record rates. The debate is over what that usage is worth.
