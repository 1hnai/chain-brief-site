Crypto's most consequential piece of U.S. legislation in years is alive, barely, and running out of calendar. The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act -- known as the CLARITY Act -- passed the House last July by a bipartisan 294-134 margin, then stalled in the Senate for months. Now, with Congress returning from recess and a Senate Banking Committee markup tentatively set for May, the industry is watching a tight window that may not reopen for years.

## What the CLARITY Act Actually Does

The bill creates a three-bucket regulatory framework for digital assets. Tokens that function as securities fall under SEC oversight. Tokens that function as commodities fall under the CFTC. Stablecoins get a joint federal regime. The goal is to end the decade-long jurisdictional war between the two agencies -- a conflict that has left exchanges, DeFi protocols, and token issuers guessing about which rules apply to them on any given day.

For the industry, clarity on this question is not academic. Projects cannot list tokens without knowing whether they are selling securities. Exchanges cannot custody assets without knowing which regulator can shut them down. Institutional capital sitting on the sidelines often cites regulatory uncertainty as the reason it stays there.

## The Stablecoin Yield Problem

The bill's biggest snag heading into April was the treatment of stablecoin yield. Crypto companies want to offer interest or rewards on stablecoin holdings to compete with money market funds. Banks argue that letting stablecoins pay yield without FDIC insurance or reserve requirements would pull deposits out of the traditional banking system.

A compromise emerged in March and held through recess: passive yield from simply holding a stablecoin would be prohibited, but rewards tied to payments, transfers, or on-chain activity would be allowed. That distinction matters enormously. It lets Circle, Paxos, and similar issuers build reward programs without triggering bank-style deposit rules. The banking lobby accepted the language, and that cleared what had been the single biggest holdup.

## What Is Still Unresolved

The stablecoin yield issue was roughly 99 percent resolved by the time Congress left for Easter recess. What replaced it as the main sticking point was a side deal: a Republican senator sought to attach community bank deregulation provisions to the bill as the price of cooperation. Crypto advocates worry that loading unrelated provisions onto the bill makes it harder to move quickly and hands opponents more reasons to vote no.

White House crypto adviser Bo Hines told reporters in mid-April that most remaining hurdles were being cleared, and the administration is actively engaged. But the bill still needs committee approval before it can come to a full Senate floor vote.

## Why the May Window Matters So Much

The math is stark. If the Senate Banking Committee does not mark up the bill in May, the odds of a floor vote before the summer recess drop sharply. After that, Congress turns its attention to the November 2026 midterms. Senator Cynthia Lummis, one of the bill's principal sponsors, has said publicly that failure to pass CLARITY this year means waiting until at least 2030, when a new Congress would need to restart the entire legislative process from scratch.

Galaxy Research puts the odds of CLARITY becoming law in 2026 at roughly 50-50, and possibly lower. That estimate has not improved much despite the stablecoin compromise, because the Senate floor calendar is genuinely crowded and crypto legislation does not have automatic priority over budget, defense, and healthcare fights.

## What Passage Would Mean for Markets

A signed CLARITY Act would immediately resolve several open questions that currently suppress institutional participation. Spot ETF approvals for assets beyond Bitcoin and Ethereum have been held up partly because the SEC lacks a clear statutory mandate for how to treat altcoins. DeFi protocols cannot build U.S.-facing products with confidence when the SEC might classify their governance tokens as unregistered securities at any point.

Tokenized real-world assets, which hit $27 billion in 2026 and are growing fast, would benefit from a predictable legal framework for what counts as a security token and what counts as a commodity token. BlackRock's BUIDL fund, Franklin Templeton's on-chain money market vehicles, and a wave of bank-issued stablecoins are all waiting for this clarity before expanding further.

## The Alternative

If CLARITY fails, the status quo continues: fragmented state-level regulation, enforcement-driven policy from federal agencies, and an ongoing drain of crypto development activity to more permissive jurisdictions. The EU's MiCA framework is already operational. The UK and Singapore have functioning licensing regimes. Every quarter the U.S. delays is a quarter those frameworks embed themselves further as the global default.

The next four weeks in the Senate will likely determine whether the U.S. shapes crypto's regulatory future or inherits someone else's version of it.
